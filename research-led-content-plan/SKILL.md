# Research-led content plan

The failure this prevents: a calendar built from what the product team is proud of, which reliably omits the thing customers actually love and use most.

The fix is an order-of-operations rule. Signals first, themes second, calendar third. Never the reverse.

## Inputs

Read `company-context.md` first (repo root, or search the workspace for it) and use the fields that apply to this skill. If the file or a line is missing, ask once, then offer to save the answer back to it. Never fill a gap with an assumption about the company.

## Step 1: Build the signal list before touching a calendar

Read the buyer research, win/loss notes, support themes and sales-call material. Extract every signal: what buyers raise unprompted, what they react hardest to, what they complain about, what they ask for.

For each signal record:

- **What the signal is**, in the buyer's framing rather than the product's.
- **Evidence strength.** How many accounts, how senior, how recent.
- **Whether it is a stated preference or a demonstrated behaviour.** These are different and the second is stronger.

Rank by evidence strength. Unprompted beats prompted. Recent beats old. Multiple accounts beat one vivid quote.

## Step 2: Cross-check the list against real usage

Pull whatever usage data exists: what features get used most, by how many accounts, how often.

**Then compare the two lists.** The gap between them is the most valuable finding in the whole exercise.

The characteristic result is a capability that is both the strongest delight signal in the research and the top real usage, which appears nowhere in the current marketing narrative. It is usually something unglamorous that the product team stopped talking about years ago.

If research and usage disagree with the existing narrative, the disagreement reshapes the calendar. Do not note it in a footer and then build the calendar from the narrative anyway.

## Step 3: Group into themes

A theme is a buyer question, not a product area. "Can it actually do things" is a theme. "Integrations" is a product area.

Themes should sequence into an argument across the weeks. A workable default arc: what it is, what it does, what it resolves, why you can trust it, with the ambition payoff last.

## Step 4: Check the doctrine before writing anything

- **Content-type mix.** Whatever ratio the house targets between point-of-view content, product content and customer proof. If no customer story is cleared, the proof layer is impossible and the plan runs product-heavy. Say that explicitly rather than letting it pass unremarked.
- **Claim tiering.** Check whether any claim has been withheld pending evidence. Where source documents contradict each other on this, write to the claim without attaching the evidence you do not have, and flag the contradiction for fixing at source.
- **Consent.** Assume no customer quote, name or logo may be used unless explicit sign-off exists. Buyer language shapes the copy with names stripped.
- **Format and cadence.** Whatever the channel research says about post types, length, timing and link placement.

## Step 5: Write the calendar with constraints inline

Under each post, write the constraints as instructions to whoever designs and schedules it:

- What a slide or line must not become.
- What figure must not be added, and what must be verified before it runs.
- Where an element is genuinely optional, and the reason, so the person can make the call.

**The person building the asset will not read a separate constraints appendix.** Put the constraint next to the thing it governs.

Carry a short standing-rules block at the top for the rules applying to every post: consent, competitor handling, the never-say list.

**On naming other companies:** saying you connect to another system is a statement about your own product. Describing what that system is like to use is characterisation of another vendor. If house rules forbid the second, the research will be full of tempting material and none of it may reach a post.

## Step 6: Produce a signal-to-post map

A table: signal, evidence strength, which post carries it.

Every post traces to a row. Anything with no row does not get a post. This is the artifact that makes the plan defensible when someone asks why a particular feature is missing.

## Step 7: Split the plan from its context

Two documents.

**The plan** is the calendar, the copy and the inline constraints. It is what gets scheduled and designed.

**The context document** carries the signal-to-post map with evidence strengths, the research-versus-usage gap, the doctrine conflicts and how each was resolved, the topics considered and dropped with reasons, the evidence behind each constraint, and the checks run.

## Step 8: Verify mechanically

Run a literal search on the finished plan for house style violations, after the last edit. See `copy-register-check`.

Then check: no uncleared quote, name or logo; no competitor characterised, including implicitly through a silent subject; every figure and timeline matches the current approved number; capability and channel gaps respected; every post traceable to a signal row.

## Step 9: Name the one thing that would change the plan

Close with what is missing. It is usually a single piece of evidence, most often a cleared customer story, which is worth more than the whole calendar and unlocks the claim the product rests on. Say so plainly, and name who would need to clear it.
