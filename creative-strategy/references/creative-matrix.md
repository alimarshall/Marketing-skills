# The creative matrix: deployment, diversity, volume and offer

Where the pieces get combined into a plan, and where the plan gets priced before anyone writes copy.

The order matters. Most creative programmes write the concepts and then discover the budget cannot read them. Do the arithmetic in section 4 first, then fill the matrix to the size the arithmetic allows.

---

## 1. The matrix

```
Pain / desire territory
  × Avatars holding it
    × Angle
      × Awareness state
        × Mechanic
          × Hook
            × Format
```

Its purpose is coverage, not volume. Filling it systematically is what stops a set being seven executions of whatever the team thought of first.

You will not run every cell and should not try. The matrix is a map of what exists so that the cut is a decision rather than an accident. Fill it wide, then cut hard, then write down what you cut.

In practice: eight to fifteen angle territories surfaced, three to five carried forward, and **three to five concepts in total**, sized by section 4. One concept per angle is the default and a second is earned. The count that governs is section 4's, because a LinkedIn campaign carries two ads: a five-concept set is a sequence across campaigns, not a single test.

**A naming collision worth knowing.** "Creative matrix" also names a design-thinking ideation method: a five-by-five grid, people categories against enabling solutions, one idea per cell, timeboxed to twenty minutes, quantity over quality. Different tool, same name. It is a genuinely good way to *generate* the cells of this matrix in a room with other people, so it is worth knowing rather than avoiding. Just do not conflate the two in a document.

---

## 2. Angle across awareness states

The governing rule, and the one that most improves a set for the least work:

**The angle stays the same. What changes is how it is expressed.**

An angle is not tied to an awareness state. Every angle carried forward should get a deployment read across all five, because the same argument at a different state is a different ad, a different audience and often a different format. This is the cheapest expansion available: one angle, correctly deployed, populates a whole funnel without inventing a second argument.

Work it as a grid. Angle down the side, the five states across the top, and in each cell what the ad opens with, the audience it runs to, and the format that fits.

| State | Opens with | LinkedIn audience | Format that tends to fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Specific observation, number, identity call-out | Cold, broad function and seniority | Statistic, single image, Thought Leader Ad |
| Problem-aware | The pain named once, flatly, and its cost | Cold ICP, function and skills | Single image, document ad, TLA |
| Solution-aware | The mechanism, and why that shape | Content and video engagers | Explainer, carousel, demo, document ad |
| Product-aware | Proof, or the objection actually held | Site and engagement retargeting | Case study, screenshot, comparison, conversation ad |
| Most aware | The offer, the trigger, the low-friction path | Demo-page and pricing-page retargeting | Single image with lead form, conversation ad |

Two things to record in the cells rather than leave implicit.

**Which cells you cannot currently fill, and why.** Product-aware and most-aware cells lean on proof, so where your proof is missing, unverified or blocked by consent, those cells stay empty. Name them and name the blocker, because it converts a creative problem into a specific unblock with an owner.

**Market sophistication, separately.** Awareness is what this buyer knows. Sophistication is how tired the market is of the claim. Establish where your category's claims sit and write it down, because it decides what a hook is allowed to do. Late in a market a bare claim carries no information, and only a mechanism, a specific number with its denominator, or a reframe of what the category is for still moves. Newer claim types sit earlier and age fast. A claim that would have worked at low sophistication does not read as bold when deployed late, it reads as noise.

## 3. The diversity check

**There is no platform or industry creative-diversity score.** Meta publishes guidance on diversification and does not score it; the scores that circulate are vendor constructs. So this check is defined here, and should be described as ours rather than as a standard.

Six dimensions. Run the finished set against all six before it enters a brief.

| Dimension | The test | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **Argument** | How many distinct angles? Would two concepts be defeated by the same objection? | Two concepts collapse to one argument |
| **Force** | Does the set cover more than Push? | Everything is pain-led |
| **Seat** | How many buying-committee seats are addressed? | Every concept aims at the champion |
| **Awareness** | How many states, and does each concept's hook match its state? | The whole set is written for problem-aware cold |
| **Mechanic and format** | How many structural moves, how many containers? | Five statics with a headline and a stat |
| **Visual register** | Product screenshot, data, illustration, human, text-only: how many? | One visual template recoloured |

**The threshold.** A set of three to five concepts should span at least three arguments, two forces, two seats, two awareness states, three mechanics or formats, and two visual registers. Below that it is a wording test wearing a concept test's clothes.

**The counterfeit to watch for.** "Different visuals" that look very similar do not count as diversity, on Meta's own account of it. The same is true of arguments: three concepts that all end at "we do this and it works" are one concept.

**Why diversity rather than precision.** Two independent reasons, and both hold at B2B scale. Creative contributes a large share of variance in advertising outcomes, so spread beats polish when you cannot predict. And at Meta's current delivery model, creative differentiation is itself a ranking input rather than a nicety. On LinkedIn the mechanism is different but the conclusion survives: with a few hundred clicks per ad set per week, a set that varies one thing produces one weak read, while a set that varies three produces three.

**Do not import the volume half of the diversity argument.** The published concept-count tables, which scale from single-figure counts a month at low spend to dozens at six figures, are DTC and ecommerce throughout, aggregator-sourced, and not independently verified. The arithmetic does not survive the CPM difference. Section 4.

## 4. Volume, and the channel branch

This is where the DTC canon and the LinkedIn reality separate, and the honest answer is that they are two disciplines sharing a job title. Branch, do not average.

### LinkedIn, the primary channel here

The constraint, stated plainly: sharply targeted B2B audiences run at high CPMs and produce a few hundred clicks per ad set per week. At that volume extra variants cost money and return nothing readable, because impressions spread unevenly and no arm reaches a countable number.

The practitioner discipline that follows, from the largest documented LinkedIn-only agency practice. These are working heuristics from one credible source, not laws, and the first thing to do when account history exists is check them against it:

- **Two ads per campaign.** Not the platform's own suggestion of two to four. More variants dilute the test.
- **Two full weeks minimum.** Whole weekly cycles, because days of the week behave differently and a partial week skews the read.
- **Do not compute significance inside the first week.** A handful of conversions produces false confidence.
- **Call it at 80 to 85 per cent confidence** if a variant wins consistently across two weeks. Waiting for 95 per cent at this volume means never deciding.
- **Metric order:** click-through rate reaches a readable number first, conversion rate is the most actionable, then lead quality, then cost per click. Cost per qualified opportunity is the real objective and it will not be readable inside a single test.
- **Separate the test types.** A copy test holds the visual constant. A visual test holds intro and headline identical. An offer test holds the message and swaps what is being offered.
- **The rule of threes.** Three failed tests against one offer means the offer lacks fit, not the creative. Three failed offers means look at targeting or at the product message.

Published thresholds of a hundred conversions per variant are arithmetically out of reach here and should be treated as aspirational rather than operational.

### Meta, as the secondary channel

The same method, a different feed. What changes is context, not process: polish is penalised and native is rewarded, video carries far more weight, and volume thresholds finally become reachable, which is the point at which variable-level testing and the DTC cadence literature start to apply. The concept-count tables and the weekly production conveyor belong here, not on LinkedIn. Note also that the old six-ads-per-ad-set recommendation was withdrawn, and that text-only and low-production assets outperform high-production ones in the largest available Meta dataset, which cuts against instinct.

### The arithmetic to run out loud

Before writing a single concept:

```
monthly creative budget ÷ variants ÷ weeks-in-flight = spend per variant per week
```

Compare that against what the decision needs. A directional click-through read needs roughly a low four-figure sum per variant across four weeks. A cost-per-lead read needs conversion counts that are almost certainly unreachable. If the arithmetic fails there are exactly two honest options: fewer variants, or a smaller question. Adding budget is a third option and it belongs to whoever owns the budget, not to the brief.

Do this before the concepts, not after. A set of twelve concepts handed to a budget that can read three is a wasted week for everyone.

### No silent caps

If the set is trimmed for budget, sampling or production capacity, say what was dropped and why. A truncated set presented without its truncation reads as complete coverage.

## 5. Refresh and rotation

Sources conflict here and the conflict appears to be real rather than an error, so branch by format rather than picking a number.

**Company-page creative** to a fixed audience: refresh in the four to six week range, on constant exposure. Frequency, not calendar, is the trigger.

**Thought Leader Ads** behave differently, because they borrow organic post mechanics. The best available read, a single agency's book of business, has click-through peaking somewhere around weeks ten to twelve and declining materially by around week nineteen. Treat the shape as the finding and the week numbers as approximate. Either way, rotating a TLA at six weeks throws away its best period: six weeks is the minimum, not the maximum.

Two further TLA findings worth carrying, both from a single agency's book and so directional rather than settled. The format's click-through advantage over other formats is large. And the budget curve is not monotonic: a quarter to a half of LinkedIn budget in TLAs performs best, and pushing past half inflates cost per click sharply through audience exhaustion. Treat the shape as the finding and the magnitudes as uncertain.

**Before any refresh, confirm it is fatigue.** A creative that never worked is not fatigued, and re-skinning it wastes the slot. The `creative-testing` skill has the diagnostic; stop at the first gate that failed.

## 6. The offer

The largest untreated lever in this system, and it sits above creative concept in order of leverage. An offer change moves who responds. Creative changes how many respond to the same thing.

Check whether an offer library exists before you start. If it does not, building one is a task rather than a paragraph, and until it exists every angle test runs against a fixed and unexamined offer, which means a flat result may be an offer problem misread as a creative problem. Say so in the brief.

What an offer library needs, per offer: what it is, which awareness state it fits, the friction it asks for, which avatar it is credible to, what it has produced historically, and whether it exists yet or is a proposal.

The offer types available in a B2B SaaS motion, roughly by friction:

| Offer | Friction | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| A named, specific document ad, ungated | None | Cold, unaware and problem-aware. Reach and authority. |
| A gated asset with real perceived value | A form | Problem and solution-aware. Native forms convert far better than an off-platform landing page. |
| A webinar or event with a real date | Time | Solution-aware. The only place a genuine deadline exists, which makes it the only place urgency is available. |
| A calculator or diagnostic | Effort | Solution-aware. Produces a qualifying signal as a side effect. |
| A demo | High | Product-aware and most-aware only. |
| A comparison or migration page | Low | Product-aware. Needs the page to exist first. |

Two practical notes. Document ads expose per-page viewing depth, which is a rare intent signal at low volume and worth designing the asset around. And the rule of threes in section 4 is the mechanism that tells you the offer is the problem, so record which offer each test ran against or the signal is unrecoverable.

**Where a concept depends on a page or an asset that does not exist**, that is a dependency, not a detail. It goes in the brief's dependency register with an owner. The usual failure mode is briefs outrunning production and approvals, with concepts sitting unlaunched on missing pages and pending sign-offs rather than on weak arguments.
