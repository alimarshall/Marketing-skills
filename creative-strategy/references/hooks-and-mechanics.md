# Hooks and creative mechanics

The layer between "we have chosen an angle" and "here is the copy". It is the layer most creative work skips, which is why so many concept sets turn out to be one argument written five ways.

---

## 1. The layer stack

Five layers. They are commonly collapsed into two, and the collapse is where sets lose their diversity.

| Layer | Answers | Lifespan | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Angle** | Why should this person care | Weeks to months. Campaign level. | `angle-library.md` |
| **Mechanic** | How does the ad structurally deliver the argument | Reusable indefinitely | This file, section 3 |
| **Hook** | What do they see or read in the first three seconds | Days to weeks. Rotates to fight fatigue. | This file, section 2 |
| **Format** | What container carries it | Per concept | `format-library.md` |
| **Claim** | What exactly are we promising | Must be substantiated before it runs | Your claim verification tracker |

Two consequences worth holding onto.

**Angles and hooks are tested differently.** Test angles against each other. Iterate hooks inside a winning angle. Running five hooks off one angle and calling it creative testing is the most common wasted test in the discipline, because whatever wins tells you about wording and nothing about the argument.

**The claim carries the risk, not the hook.** A hook is a delivery decision. A claim is a factual assertion with a verification status. Mark them separately, because a brief can pass a copy review and still contain an unsubstantiated claim inside a clever hook.

"Concept" sits across the stack rather than inside it: the concept is the briefable bundle, one angle plus one mechanic plus one hook plus one format plus one avatar plus its proof. There is no industry agreement on where concept belongs in the ladder, so we define it as the unit a designer can build from and leave it there.

## 2. Hook tactics

A hook expresses one angle at one awareness state. Same angle, different awareness state, different hook.

Below is the working taxonomy, each tactic paired with the trigger it pulls. The availability marks are not stylistic preference: they flag the tactics most commonly constrained by a brand's own register, legal or consent rules. **Check every mark against your own brand voice and writing rules (see `inputs.md`) and re-mark it where yours differ.** A tactic listed here as unavailable may be open to you, and one listed as available may be closed.

### Commonly available, and under-used

| Tactic | Trigger | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Demographic call-out** | Identity | Names the audience to qualify relevance. Often the highest-value tactic available, because avatars are frequently best predicted by an attribute the platform cannot target. The copy does the qualifying the targeting cannot. "Heads of [function] at 200-person companies:" |
| **Confession** | Relatability | An admission against your own interest. Publishing a limitation is the cheapest credibility purchase available and almost nobody in any category does it. Substitute, do not hedge. |
| **Explainer** | Curiosity | Names the mechanism. The tactic technical buyers weight most, and the only proof type available before customer sign-off exists. |
| **Statistic** | Credibility | Requires a denominator and a verification clearance. A round number with no denominator reads as marketing. |
| **If-then** | Identity, stakes | Qualifies and states the consequence in one line without agitating. Fits growth-led register rules unusually well. |
| **Question** | Curiosity gap | Works when the question is one the buyer cannot answer comfortably and would not have thought to ask. Fails when the answer is obviously your product. |
| **Directive** | Stakes | A flat instruction. Reads as confidence rather than persuasion. |
| **How-to** | Curiosity | Fits document ads and Thought Leader Ads where the value is delivered before any ask. |
| **Listicle** | Curiosity | Fine as structure, weak as a hook on its own. Published Meta reads put polished listicles among the weaker performers; treat the same way on LinkedIn. |
| **Reasons why** | Credibility | Enumerated justification. Good for the gatekeeper seat. |
| **Contrast** | Pattern interrupt | Commonly constrained to the narrow form in section 3. See the Contrast Without Comment entry. |
| **Warning** | Stakes | Available when it names a specific operational consequence flatly. Not available as generalised fear. |
| **Bold claim** | Pattern interrupt | Available only where proof strength matches claim strength. Shrink the claim until the proof holds it. |
| **Storytelling** | Relatability | Strong in Thought Leader Ads, weak as a cold hook: long-form storytelling as an opener is among the worst-performing TLA patterns. |
| **Relatability** | Relatability | The specific working moment, named. Where the register caps pain-led work, the moment is evidence, never the emotional centre. |
| **Direct address** | Identity | Plain second person. Underrated and always available. |
| **Challenge** | Stakes | Available as a challenge to a belief, not to the reader's competence. |
| **Call-to-action first** | Stakes | Available. Rarely the best use of the first line at cold. |

### Commonly available with a hard constraint

| Tactic | Constraint |
|---|---|
| **Contrarian** | Where your rules forbid characterising other vendors, this must be a first-person thesis about your own approach. "We don't think capability is the hard problem. Explanation is." Not a claim about what other vendors or "the market" do. |
| **Myth busting** | Same. The myth must be a belief in the market, stated without a vendor as its implied subject. If the sentence still works with "our competitors" silently inserted as the subject, rewrite it. |
| **Social proof** | Gated by consent, not by taste. Where the bank holds no externally cleared quote, name or number, this tactic is unavailable in customer-facing copy however good the material is. Check your consent position before planning a set around it. |
| **Authority** | Available for founder and practitioner authority. Not available as "leading platform" style self-assertion, which most banned-phrase lists catch and which reads as a hedge. |
| **Risk reversal** | Available where a real mechanism backs it: an autonomy dial, reversible actions, mandatory approval. Not available as reassurance language, since most enterprise registers reject "peace of mind" and "nothing to hide". |
| **Reverse psychology** | Available in principle. Almost always reads as a device at enterprise register. Use sparingly and check it against your rules on tone. |
| **Aspirational** | Available and usually on-strategy, since a growth-led register wants the leading frame first. The failure mode is fluff: an aspiration that asserts nothing fails most rules on specificity. |

### Commonly unavailable

Keep these in the taxonomy so they are recognised in a teardown. Whether each is actually closed to you is a question for your own rules.

| Tactic | Constraint category, and the reasoning |
|---|---|
| **Urgency** | Register or brand rule, plus the banned-phrase sweep. No manufactured deadlines. Available only for a real dated event: a webinar, a conference, a deadline that exists. |
| **FOMO** | Register or brand rule, as normally written, since the pressure comes from what peers or the market are doing, which is vendor characterisation by implication. |
| **Exclusivity** | Register or brand rule. Reads as downmarket-warm or as gatekeeping, and rarely matches an enterprise register. |
| **Price anchor** | Strategy, not rule. Where the research treats pricing as a conversation no ad can answer, it is excluded as an ad territory. Confirm that read before spending a concept on it. |
| **Offer only** | Not blocked by a rule. Unavailable wherever you have no offer that carries an ad on its own. Revisit if that changes. See `creative-matrix.md` section 6. |
| **Shocking statement** | Legal or substantiation, not register. A shock with no substantiated number behind it is the fastest route to a pulled ad. |

**On tactic performance data.** Published hit-rate rankings by hook tactic exist for Meta at scale, and the largest of them puts offer-only, confession, curiosity and bold claim near the top. Treat both the ranking and the dataset size as vendor-published and unaudited. It also does not transfer: it is Meta, DTC-weighted, seasonally skewed, and several of those tactics will be unavailable to any given brand. Use it to widen the tactic list you consider, never as a ranking to obey.

## 3. Creative mechanics

A mechanic is the structural move the ad makes on the viewer. It is independent of angle and of format: the same argument delivered through two different mechanics produces two genuinely different ads, which is the cheapest source of real diversity in a set.

Eight mechanics. The fit marks below are the common case. Re-derive them against your own rules on first use.

**The Implied Answer.** Pose something that sounds like an observation rather than a question, and let the visual answer it. The viewer reaches the conclusion themselves, which bypasses the scepticism a direct claim triggers. *Strong fit* wherever buyers have built verification habits, because a conclusion they reach is not a claim they have to check.

**The Social Witness.** A third party notices the change and validates it. Impact proven by someone else's recognition rather than by self-report. *Commonly blocked by consent rather than by register.* It is the mechanic most competitive sets run on named logos. Worth naming in a brief as blocked, because it makes the sign-off unblock concrete rather than administrative.

**The Reframe.** Validate a belief the buyer already holds, then turn the perspective. Manufactures the "I had not thought about it that way" reaction. *Strong fit,* and it is the mechanic that makes a contrarian angle work without characterising a competitor, because the belief being reframed is the buyer's own rather than a vendor's position.

**The Trojan Horse.** Reads as education or entertainment until the product arrives as the resolution. *Strong fit for document ads and Thought Leader Ads,* where the value is delivered before any ask and links in the post body actively hurt performance.

**Contrast Without Comment.** Two realities side by side, no editorial judgement. Juxtaposition carries the evidence. *Constrained.* Available when both sides are yours: a screen before and after, a process with and without an approval step. Not available when one side is a competitor's world, wherever your rules forbid characterising vendors, and not available when the "before" side is admin burden used as a contrast device against the reader, wherever the register caps pain-led work. State the specific once, flatly, and move to the resolution.

**The Overheard Conversation.** Framed as unintended: a message thread, a DM, a group chat. Removes the ad filter. *Available, and a strong fit for any avatar whose pain arrives as messages,* the one who is the help desk and whose queue is their DMs. The thread is the evidence. Handle with care at enterprise register and never fabricate a real-looking internal message from a named company.

**This and a.** Product placed beside something aspirational so meaning emerges through association. *Weak fit* for most B2B programmes. It is a brand-advertising mechanic that needs frequency B2B budgets do not buy, and at enterprise register it tends to produce fluff.

**The Borrowed Enemy.** Describe a problem so vividly that it unmistakably identifies a competitor without naming them, and let the viewer make the connection. *Unavailable wherever your rules forbid characterising other vendors:* not named, not unnamed. It is worth knowing the mechanic exists and worth recognising it in a competitor teardown, because it is the dominant displacement move in most categories and you will see it run against you.

There is a permitted neighbour, and the distinction is narrow enough to state carefully: describing a **product type or an architecture** rather than a vendor. "Built for a team running three separate systems of record" describes a situation. Anything that reads as a characterisation of who sold them that situation does not. When in doubt, apply the swap test: insert "our competitors" as the silent subject and see whether the sentence still works. If it does, it is out.

## 4. Writing the hook

**Match the awareness state.** Non-negotiable, and the check that is missing from most reviews.

| State | Open with | Never open with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | A specific observation, a number, an identity call-out | The product name |
| Problem-aware | The pain named precisely, once, and what it costs | A feature |
| Solution-aware | The mechanism. How this works and why that shape | Category benefits everyone claims |
| Product-aware | Proof, or the objection they are actually holding | The problem they moved past weeks ago |
| Most aware | The offer, the trigger, the low-friction path | Education |

Two failures account for most wasted B2B spend and both are mismatches: cold creative that opens with a product name, and retargeting creative that opens with a generic pain the viewer solved three weeks ago. Test for this explicitly in the verification checklist.

**Write it word for word.** "An attention-grabbing opener" is not a hook. A brief containing a description of a hook rather than a hook has not been finished.

**Respect the fold.** Feed formats truncate at roughly **150 characters**, so the argument has to survive the cut. Thought Leader Ads carry up to 3,000 characters with no headline and no CTA button, and they truncate at the same place: roughly three rendered lines, about 150 characters, then "see more". The extra 2,850 characters are the body, not the hook. Check the format before you write, and validate every count against `platform-specs.md`.

**Specificity filters better than targeting settings do.** A concrete job description outperforms a benefit claim, and no amount of audience configuration rescues a generic sentence. This is the same point the avatar targeting gap makes from the other direction.

**One avatar, one pain, one line.** Two use cases in one sentence is the most common B2B hook failure.

## 5. Building the set

For each angle carried forward, vary the mechanic before you vary the wording. A set of three concepts on one angle should read as three different ads, not three drafts.

A practical shape that produces real diversity without inflating volume:

- One concept using a **mechanism-forward** mechanic (Implied Answer or an Explainer hook), aimed at the solution-aware state
- One using a **Reframe**, aimed at problem-aware cold, carrying the contrarian angle as a first-person thesis
- One using **Confession** or a published limitation, aimed at the gatekeeper seat

Three mechanics, three awareness states, three seats, one angle. That is a readable test. Three stat cards is not.

Then check the whole set against the diversity dimensions in `creative-matrix.md` section 3 before it goes into a brief.
